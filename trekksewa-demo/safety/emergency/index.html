Aid posts on the trail

HRA Pheriche aid post 4,240 mSpring and autumn trekking seasons only, roughly March to May and late September or early October to the end of November. The autumn 2026 post opened in early October. Volunteer doctors give round-the-clock emergency care in season and a daily talk on altitude illness (at 3 pm in spring 2026). Foreign trekkers are normally charged for a consultation, so bring cash and your insurance details. The HRA's own material gives the height as about 4,250 m.
HRA Manang aid post 3,550 mSpring and autumn trekking seasons only, on the same pattern as Pheriche. The autumn 2026 post opened in early October. The same service on the Annapurna Circuit, in the village where most trekkers take a rest day before the Thorong La. Go to the afternoon altitude talk even if you feel fine.
Everest ER (HRA base camp clinic) 5,364 mSpring climbing season only, about April and May. A tent clinic the HRA has run at base camp each spring since 2003, mainly for expedition teams and their staff. We found no announcement of an autumn clinic, so do not count on it outside the climbing season.

Numbers checked 2026-11. Kathmandu landlines changed format in recent years, so an old card or guidebook may show numbers that no longer connect. Save these to your phone before you leave the city.

How rescue works in Nepal

  1. Nepal has no public mountain-rescue service and no free air ambulance. Evacuations are flown by private helicopter companies, and a pilot will not normally take off until someone has guaranteed the bill: your insurer, your trekking agency, or you with a card or cash deposit.

  2. The usual chain is short. You, your guide or a lodge owner calls your trekking agency. The agency, or you, calls the 24-hour assistance number on your insurance policy with your policy number, location and symptoms, and the insurer's medical team decides whether the flight is covered. If it is, the insurer sends the helicopter operator a guarantee of payment, or the agency fronts the cost and claims it back. Speak to the insurer before the helicopter is booked whenever you can: a flight they did not authorise may not be reimbursed.

  3. Without insurance, or if the insurer says no, the operator still wants paying. Published estimates for 2025–26 run from about USD 3,000 to 6,000 from the upper Khumbu, USD 2,500 to 4,000 from Annapurna Base Camp to Pokhara and USD 2,500 to 5,500 from Langtang to Kathmandu. Some quotes for Gorak Shep and Everest Base Camp reach USD 8,000 to 12,000. The UK government puts the price at GBP 1,000 to 2,000 or more per flying hour. Hospital bills come on top.

  4. Check your policy before you leave home. It has to name helicopter evacuation and cover trekking to the highest point on your route, which means cover to about 6,000 m for Everest Base Camp, Gokyo or the Thorong La. Many standard and credit-card policies stop at 3,000 m or lower. Keep the policy number and the assistance phone number on paper as well as on your phone, and give a copy to your guide or agency.

  5. Helicopters fly in daylight and in flyable weather only, so a flight requested in the afternoon often arrives the next morning, and cloud or wind can hold it for a day or more. Do not wait at altitude for one if the patient is getting worse and can still be moved. Going down, on foot, on a horse or carried, is the treatment for altitude illness. In season the Himalayan Rescue Association doctors at Pheriche and Manang can assess you and say whether a flight is needed.

  6. With no phone signal, send the fastest fit person, ideally your guide, to the nearest lodge with a working phone, Wi-Fi or satellite phone. Lodge owners make these calls every season, and police, army and national park posts usually have radios. Send a written note with the patient's name, nationality, passport number, insurer, policy number and assistance phone, plus the exact place, the symptoms and the time. On remote routes such as Manaslu, Kanchenjunga and Dolpo, carry a satellite messenger. Under the March 2026 permit rules, the agency that obtains a restricted-area permit for a solo trekker carries the responsibility for emergency rescue.

  7. Be aware of rescue fraud. Police say unnecessary or inflated helicopter evacuations have been billed to foreign insurers for years. In January 2026 the Central Investigation Bureau arrested managers of rescue companies, and in March more than 30 people were charged over roughly USD 19.7 million in claims. Do not agree to fly out for mild symptoms that settle with rest or descent unless a doctor advises it, turn down any offer of a ride home on your insurance, and if you share a flight tell your insurer how many patients were on board. Since 2018 helicopter companies, hospitals and agencies have had to report each rescue to the Department of Tourism and the Tourist Police, and insurers check the paperwork.

What to tell them

  • Who you are, your nationality and how many people need help.
  • Exactly where you are: coordinates, the nearest village or lodge, and which side of the valley.
  • What is wrong, since when, and whether the person can walk.
  • Your insurer, policy number and its 24-hour assistance phone.
  • A phone number or a lodge name where you can be reached, and the weather where you are.
