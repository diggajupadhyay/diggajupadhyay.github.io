Trekking safety in Nepal

Most trekkers who get into trouble in Nepal are fit, well equipped and going too high too fast. Three tools and a few rules cover most of what goes wrong.

The three rules of altitude

If you remember nothing else

  1. 1

    If you feel unwell up high, it is altitude until proven otherwise

    A headache with nausea, tiredness or dizziness above 2,500 m is altitude sickness. Do not explain it away as a cold, the food or the sun.

  2. 2

    Never go higher with symptoms

    Stay where you are until they clear. A day lost to rest is cheaper than a helicopter.

  3. 3

    If you are getting worse, go down now

    Not after breakfast, not when the group is ready. Descending 500 to 1,000 m is the treatment, and it works.

Three illnesses, one cause

All come from going higher than your body has adapted to. The first is common and a warning. The other two can kill within hours.

Common

AMS: acute mountain sickness

Headache plus at least one of: loss of appetite or nausea, unusual tiredness, dizziness. Usually starts 6 to 12 hours after arriving higher.

Do: stop ascending, rest, drink, take a simple painkiller. Go down if it is no better in a day or gets worse.

Emergency

HACE: swelling of the brain

AMS that progresses to staggering, confusion, odd behaviour or drowsiness. The heel-to-toe walking test fails.

Do: descend immediately with help, day or night. Give oxygen if there is any. Call for evacuation.

Emergency

HAPE: fluid in the lungs

Breathless at rest, a wet cough, sometimes frothy or pink spit, a tight chest and a sudden drop in performance. Can occur without AMS first.

Do: descend immediately with as little exertion as possible. Keep warm, give oxygen. Call for evacuation.

Going up sensibly

  • Above about 3,000 m, raise your sleeping altitude by no more than roughly 500 m a day.
  • Add a night with no gain for every 1,000 m you climb.
  • Climb high in the day if you like, but sleep low.
  • Avoid going from below 1,500 m to sleep above 2,800 m in a single day when you have the choice.
  • Drink enough to keep your urine pale, eat even when you are not hungry, and leave alcohol for the way down.
  • Walk slower than you think you need to. The fittest person in the group is not the safest.
  • Tell someone how you feel every evening, honestly. Hiding a headache is how people get hurt.

These figures follow the ascent-rate advice in the Wilderness Medical Society's practice guidelines. People differ: some need longer.

Medicines

Acetazolamide (Diamox) can help prevent AMS and speed acclimatization. It is a prescription medicine with side effects and is not suitable for everyone, so talk to a travel doctor before your trip rather than buying it on the trail. No medicine makes it safe to keep climbing with symptoms.

Insurance

Your policy must name helicopter evacuation and cover trekking to the highest point on your route. Many standard policies stop at 3,000 or 4,000 m. Carry the policy number and the 24-hour assistance phone on paper.

How rescue works in Nepal

Altitude zones

1,500–2,500 m
Moderate altitude. You may notice the climb, but altitude illness is unusual.
2,500–3,500 m
High altitude. Altitude illness is possible, especially after a fast ascent.
3,500–5,500 m
Very high altitude. Altitude illness is common without gradual ascent.
5,500– m
Extreme altitude. The body cannot fully adapt. Keep visits short.
