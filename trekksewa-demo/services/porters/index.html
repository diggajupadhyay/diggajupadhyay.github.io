Porters

Porters make trekking in Nepal possible, and they are the people most often short-changed by it. Hiring one fairly costs little and changes a great deal.

What a porter does

A porter carries your main bag from lodge to lodge, usually walking ahead of the group and arriving first. One porter normally carries for two trekkers. Most are farmers from the middle hills working the season, not Sherpas from the high valleys, and they feel the cold and the altitude exactly as you do.

Hiring responsibly

  • Weigh the bag. Keep your share to 10–12 kg so the total load stays at 25 kg or under. Leave what you do not need at your hotel.
  • Ask about insurance. A porter should be insured for accident and evacuation on your route. If the agency cannot confirm it, choose another agency.
  • Look at their kit on day one. Boots, a warm jacket, gloves, sunglasses and a sleeping bag or blanket for the high nights. If something is missing, it is cheap to put right in Kathmandu or Namche.
  • Check where they sleep and eat. Above the tree line that means a place indoors and hot food.
  • Watch for altitude sickness. Porters hide symptoms because they fear losing the job. Ask every evening.
  • Never send a sick porter down alone. Someone who speaks their language goes with them, with money for treatment.
  • Pay and tip directly. Hand the tip to each person yourself at the end.

Typical daily rate

About USD 18–25 a day (roughly NPR 2,700–3,800); some agencies quote up to USD 30

These are 2026 agency prices for a porter who carries 20–25 kg, usually the bags of two trekkers. As a floor, the trekking agencies' association set a minimum porter wage of NPR 2,400 a day from 17 July 2023; we found no later revision. The price normally covers the porter's own food and lodging, but agencies differ, so ask what is included and ask to see the insurance. Your tip is extra.

How much a porter may carry

30 kg is the International Porter Protection Group's ceiling for Nepal; agencies normally load 20–25 kg, usually the bags of two trekkers

The International Porter Protection Group says no porter should carry more than they safely can, with 30 kg as the most in Nepal, and that the limit should come down for altitude, trail and weather. Agencies in 2026 describe 20–25 kg a porter, which is why two trekkers sharing one are asked to keep their bags to about 10–12 kg each. We found no current Nepali rule that fixes a load for trekking porters, and the trekking agencies' association's published wage agreements do not mention one. The guideline, as the bodies we checked quote it, does not say whether the porter's own kit counts, so assume it does and pack lighter.

The IPPG guidelines

Five IPPG guidelines: proper clothing, shelter and food, insurance and equal medical care, no sick porter sent down alone, and a load within the porter's ability

The International Porter Protection Group asks that porters get clothing and footwear fit for the season and altitude, adequate shelter, food and drink, and life insurance with the same medical care you would expect yourself. A sick or injured porter must not be paid off and sent down alone: the trek leader has to be told, and someone who speaks the porter's language goes down with them. Nobody should carry more than they safely can, 30 kg at most in Nepal. Nepal's own rules for trekking agencies, as amended in 2018, also require the staff who go with you to have accident insurance of at least NPR 1,000,000 for the whole trip, so ask the agency to show you the policy.

Porters you can contact

Each one works within the fair-trek standards: a fair load, proper kit, insurance and full pay.

How to get a porter

A porter hired through a registered company or a lodge you trust is insured and has someone responsible for them. Here is the simplest way to ask.

Ask with your plan

  1. Make your plan. A few simple questions set out your dates, your route and how many you are.
  2. Send it. Press Share or Print on the plan and send it to two or three trekking companies, and ask each for a price with a porter.
  3. Compare the answers. Look at the daily rate, what it covers, the load they allow and how the porter is insured.

Check before you pay

  • Ask to see the insurance. It should cover the porter for accident and rescue on your route.
  • Agree the load. 20 to 25 kg at the very most, so about 10 to 12 kg from each of two trekkers.
  • Agree the pay. The daily rate, whether it covers the porter's food and bed, and that it is paid in full.
  • Look at the kit on day one. Boots, a warm jacket, gloves and sunglasses for the highest night.

The fair-trek standards

What every partner agrees to

Based on the International Porter Protection Group's guidelines and on how the better Nepali agencies already work. Partners accept them when they apply.

Checked credentials

Guides are listed with their licence number and agencies with their registration, tourism licence and tax numbers, so you can verify them yourself.

Loads within limits

Porters carry no more than 25 kg including their own kit, and less at altitude or on rough ground.

Wages agreed and paid in full

The daily rate, what it includes and who pays for food and lodging are agreed before the trek and paid without deductions.

Everyone insured

Guides and porters are covered for accident, medical care and evacuation for the whole route, including its highest point.

Dressed for the altitude

Staff have boots, warm clothing, a sleeping place and food suited to the highest and coldest night of the trek.

Nobody sent down alone

A sick or injured guide or porter gets the same care as a client and is never paid off and sent down unaccompanied.
