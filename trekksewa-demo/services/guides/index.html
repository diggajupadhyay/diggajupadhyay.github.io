Trekking guides

A good guide does far more than show the way. They pace the group, book the beds, read the weather, notice when you are not right and know who to call.

What a licensed guide is

In Nepal a trekking guide holds a government licence, issued after a training course and renewed periodically. The licence is personal: it has a photo, a number and an expiry date, and a guide should be happy to show it.

A porter-guide is a different arrangement: one person who carries a lighter load and also leads the way. It suits one or two fit trekkers on a well-marked route. It is a job description rather than a licence, so ask whether yours actually holds a guide licence if your route requires one.

Before you agree anything

  • See the licence card and note the number.
  • Agree the daily rate and exactly what it covers: food, lodging, transport to and from the trailhead, insurance.
  • Ask what insurance the guide has and up to what altitude.
  • Fix the number of days, and what happens if the trek runs longer or is cut short.
  • Talk about pace and rest days. A guide who promises to get you there faster is not doing you a favour.
  • Put it in a message or on paper, even if it is two lines.

Tipping

Tips are expected and are a real part of income. A common guide is about a day's wage for every week of trekking, more for a hard trip handled well, given directly to each person at the end.

Typical daily rate

About USD 25–40 a day (roughly NPR 3,800–6,100); a few agencies quote up to USD 50

These are 2026 agency prices for a licensed, English-speaking trekking guide. Most quotes are USD 25–30, rising to USD 35–40 or more for senior guides, harder routes and restricted areas. As a floor, the trekking agencies' association set a minimum guide wage of NPR 3,100 a day from 17 July 2023; we found no later revision. The price normally covers the guide's own food, lodging and insurance, but agencies word this differently, so ask. Ask too whether their transport, such as a Lukla flight, is included. Your tip is extra.

How licensing works Typical, unconfirmed

Guides qualify through a five-week trekking guide course at the Nepal Academy of Tourism and Hotel Management (NATHM) and carry a licence that has to be renewed

NATHM's 2026 notices describe a five-week trekking guide course with 300–500 places, open to applicants who have passed class 12, or class 10 with two years at a registered trekking agency. A 2012 agreement between the trekking agencies' association and the workers' unions shows that the licence has to be renewed, at that time through the tourism ministry. We could not confirm from an official page which office issues and renews it today, and the sources we found disagree, so we do not name one. Ask to see the original licence and check the name, the licence number and the expiry date. Your agency also enters the guide's details when it applies for your TIMS card.

Is a guide compulsory?

Required on paper since 1 April 2023; not applied in Khumbu, and unevenly enforced in Annapurna and Langtang

The Nepal Tourism Board's revised TIMS provision, effective 31 March 2023, says foreign trekkers on its listed national-park and conservation-area routes (Everest, Annapurna, Langtang and Manaslu among them) must walk with a licensed guide and carry a TIMS card issued by a registered agency. Khumbu Pasang Lhamu Rural Municipality rejected the rule and still registers independent trekkers at Lukla; in Annapurna and Langtang the rule stands, but 2025–26 reports conflict, with agencies saying guide licences are checked and independent accounts saying TIMS checkposts are mostly unstaffed, while restricted areas always require a guide and an agency.

How to get a guide

Most trekkers find their guide through a trekking company. Here is the simplest way to ask, and what to check.

Ask with your plan

  1. Make your plan. A few simple questions set out your dates, your route and how many you are.
  2. Send it. Press Share or Print on the plan and send it to two or three trekking companies, or to a guide someone you trust has walked with.
  3. Compare the answers. Look at the daily rate, what it covers, and who exactly will walk with you.

Check before you pay

  • See the licence card. It carries the guide's photo, a number and an expiry date.
  • Look the company up. If the guide comes through a company, find it in the trekking agencies' association member list.
  • Ask about insurance. Who insures the guide, and up to what height?
  • Agree it in writing. The days, the daily rate and what it includes. A message on your phone is enough.
