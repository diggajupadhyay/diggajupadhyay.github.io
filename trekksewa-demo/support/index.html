Help and common questions

How to plan a trek with this site, what the words mean, and how far to trust what you read here.

New to trekking?

Start with the planner. It asks three simple questions and builds your trek day by day.

Plan my trek

Trekking words explained

Trek
A walk of several days from village to village, sleeping somewhere new each night. No climbing skill is needed.
Teahouse or lodge
A simple family-run guesthouse on the trail. You get a small twin room, a shared bathroom, and meals in a dining room with a stove.
Guide
A licensed person who walks with you, knows the way, books the beds and looks after your safety.
Porter
A person who carries your main bag from lodge to lodge, so that you walk with only a small day bag.
Trekking company or agency
A registered business that arranges the whole trip for you: guide, porter, permits, transport and beds.
Permit
A paper you pay for that allows you into a national park, a conservation area or a restricted area. It is checked on the trail.
TIMS card
A registration card for trekkers, arranged through a trekking company. It records who is on which trail.
Restricted area
A border region where foreigners may only trek with a registered company and a licensed guide, on a special permit.
Altitude sickness
Feeling ill because the air is thin: headache, nausea, tiredness, poor sleep. It is common above 2,500 m and passes if you stop climbing. If it gets worse, you must go down.
Acclimatization
Giving your body time to get used to the height, by climbing slowly and taking rest days. It is the single best protection against altitude sickness.
Rest day
A day when you sleep at the same height for a second night. You usually take a short walk higher and come back down.
Pass, or "La"
The low point of a ridge where the trail crosses from one valley to the next. Often the highest point of a trek.
Base camp
The place at the foot of a big mountain where climbers start. On a trek it is a viewpoint, not a climb.
Dal bhat
Rice with lentil soup and vegetables: the everyday meal of Nepal, filling, freshly cooked and refilled for free.
Namaste
The greeting you will hear all day, said with palms together. It means "I greet you".

Planning a trek

I have never trekked before. Where do I start?

Open Plan my trek. It asks three simple questions (how many days, how much walking, which month), suggests treks that suit you, and then lays out a plan day by day.

How fit do I need to be?

For a gentle trek you should be comfortable walking four hours a day for several days in a row, with some hills. Plenty of people trek in their sixties and seventies. Walk regularly for a few months beforehand, and choose a route that stays below about 3,500 m for your first time.

I am over sixty. Is trekking in Nepal sensible?

Yes, with the right route and enough time. Talk to your doctor first, especially about your heart, lungs and any medicines. Take a guide, hire a porter, add rest days, and buy insurance that covers trekking to the height you will reach and rescue by helicopter.

Can I book a guide, a porter or a trekking company here?

You can do everything except the booking itself. Make a plan, then press Share or Print and send it to two or three trekking companies or guides to ask for a price. Our own booking desk is not open yet; here is how to check that a company is registered.

Where will I sleep?

On most treks, in lodges (also called teahouses) in the villages along the trail. The planner and each place page list the lodges that are mapped at every stop.

What will it cost?

The planner gives a range for your trek, your group and the help you choose. As a rough guide, a week on a popular lodge trek with a guide and a porter costs about USD 500 to 900 a person, not counting flights to Nepal.

Using the site

How do I make the text bigger?

Press the "Aa" button at the top of any page. Each press makes everything larger, and the third press returns to normal. Your choice is remembered. On a phone you will also find it under More.

Is it free?

Yes. Reading the guides and using every tool is free and needs no account.

Do I need to sign up?

No. Saved treks, plans, packing lists and your emergency card are stored in your browser on your own device. Nothing is sent to us.

Will my saved things appear on my other phone or laptop?

No. Because they live in your browser, each device has its own. Clearing your browser data for this site removes them.

Is there an app?

TrekkSewa is a web app. On most phones you can add it to your home screen from the browser menu and it will open full-screen like any other app.

Offline

What works without a connection?

The safety tools (altitude check, acclimatization checker, emergency page), your saved treks, and every page you have already opened. Open the pages you will need while you still have Wi-Fi, or use the “Keep offline” button on a trek.

Do the maps work offline?

Map tiles you have already looked at are kept, so pan and zoom along your route beforehand. We do not bulk-download maps, because the free map services we use do not allow it. For full offline mapping carry a dedicated app or a paper map.

Does the forecast work offline?

No. You will see the last forecast you loaded, with its time, marked as saved.

Does GPS work without signal?

Yes. Your phone finds its position from satellites, not the mobile network. Sending that position to someone does need signal or Wi-Fi.

Accuracy

How accurate are the route guides?

They are written from long familiarity with these routes and checked against published sources, but lodges close, trails move and rules change. Each trek states how well established its details are. Treat distances and times as approximate and confirm anything important locally.

How accurate are the map markers?

Most markers take their position from OpenStreetMap; the rest are our own estimates and are labelled approximate. Places we could not confirm are not drawn at all. The dashed line on a trek map simply joins the stops in order. Follow the trail on the map and on the ground, not the line.

Are permit fees current?

They were last reviewed in November 2026, and each permit shows whether we could confirm it. Fees change, sometimes mid-season, so check with the Nepal Tourism Board or your agency before you pay.

Is the altitude sickness check a diagnosis?

No. It is an educational aid based on a standard scoring system. It cannot examine you. If someone is getting worse at altitude, descend.

Guides, porters, agencies and gear

How do I get listed?

Fill in the application on the partners page. It takes about five minutes and can be completed in English or Nepali.

Does it cost anything to apply?

No.

Found a mistake?

If a stage, an altitude, a fee or a phone number is wrong, we want to know. Tell us which page, what is wrong and how you know.

A contact address for corrections will be published here when the partner network opens.

In an emergency

This page cannot help you in an emergency. Open the emergency toolkit, or call Nepal Police on 100 or the Tourist Police on 1144.
