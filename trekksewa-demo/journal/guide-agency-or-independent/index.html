Guide, agency or on your own: three ways to trek in Nepal

What the rules say, what happens in practice, and how to decide between a full-service agency, a guide you hire yourself and going independently.

How you organise a trek changes its cost, its flexibility and how much of your attention goes on logistics instead of mountains. It is also partly decided for you by the rules, which changed in 2023 and again in 2026.

What the rules say

Restricted areas. Manaslu, Tsum, Upper Mustang, Nar Phu, Dolpo, Kanchenjunga and the other restricted zones issue permits only through a registered trekking agency, and you must walk with a licensed guide. Since March 2026 a permit can be issued to a single trekker; before that the minimum was two.

Most other routes. Since 1 April 2023 the Nepal Tourism Board has required foreign trekkers to take a licensed guide, arranged through a registered agency, on its listed routes in national parks and conservation areas. In practice enforcement has been uneven, and checkpoints differ from one region and season to the next. The rule exists, and you should plan as though it will be applied to you.

The Everest region. The local municipality in Khumbu issues its own entry card at Lukla and has not applied the guide requirement, so independent trekking continues there.

Rules like these move. The permits page carries the date we last checked.

Option one: a full-service agency

You pay one price and the agency arranges permits, transport, a guide, porters, lodges and usually meals.

Good for: restricted areas, where you need an agency anyway; camping routes; short holidays where a lost day matters; anyone who does not want to spend the trek negotiating.

Costs: the most of the three, and prices for the same route vary widely. Ask what is included line by line.

Watch for: itineraries with the rest days removed to look shorter, a price far below the market, and vague answers about staff insurance and porter loads. Our agency page lists the questions to ask.

Option two: hire a guide, and a porter if you want one

You take a guide, with or without a porter, and pay your own way in the lodges. The guide can be hired through an agency, which also sorts the permits, or through a recommendation.

Good for: most people on the main routes. You keep control of pace and spending, and you have someone who knows the trail, the lodge owners and the signs of altitude sickness.

Costs: the guide's daily rate, plus a porter's if you take one, plus your own food and beds. Staff wages normally include their food and lodging; confirm that before you start.

Watch for: a guide without a licence card, no clarity on insurance, and arrangements made entirely by word of mouth. Two lines in a message, with the rate, the days and what is included, save arguments later.

Option three: independent

You carry your own pack, find your own lodges and make your own decisions.

Good for: experienced trekkers on busy, well-marked trails, mainly in the Everest region where it remains straightforward.

Costs: the least.

Watch for: the things a guide would have caught. Independent trekkers are over-represented among people who get lost in cloud, push on with altitude sickness because nobody told them to stop, or have nobody to raise the alarm. If you go alone, tell your lodge each morning where you are heading, and do not cross a pass in bad weather by yourself.

How to decide

Ask yourself three things.

  1. Is it a restricted area? Then it is an agency and a guide. Decision made.
  2. Have I been above 4,000 m before, and did I cope? If not, take a guide.
  3. What happens if I twist an ankle a day from the nearest road? If the honest answer is "I don't know", take a guide.

Whatever you choose

The people who carry and lead for you are doing a hard, seasonal job. Pay the agreed wage in full, check that they are insured and properly dressed for the highest night, keep porter loads within limits, and tip at the end. The standards we ask of our partners are a fair checklist for anyone you hire, whether or not you found them here.

Reviewed November 2026. Rules, fees and services change; confirm anything important before you rely on it.
