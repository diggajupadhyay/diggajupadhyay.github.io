The Lukla flight: why it is cancelled and how to plan for it

Why flights to the Everest region stop so often, what the Manthali arrangement means for your first night, and the options when you are stuck.

Almost every trek in the Everest region starts with a flight to Lukla, and almost every trekker who has done it has a story about waiting. The delays are not bad luck. They are built into how the airport works.

Why it stops

Lukla's runway is short, sloped and ends at a mountainside, so aircraft land uphill and take off downhill. There is no going around for a second attempt and no instrument landing system. Pilots fly by sight, which means they need to see the runway and the valley leading to it.

Cloud in the Dudh Koshi valley, cloud at the departure airport, strong wind at Lukla or poor visibility on the way will each stop flying. Conditions are usually best in the first hours after sunrise and deteriorate towards midday as the valley warms and wind and cloud build. That is why flights are scheduled from dawn and why a delay of two hours often becomes a cancellation.

The worst months are the monsoon and its shoulders, from June into September, but there is no month without cancellations.

Kathmandu or Manthali?

In the busy spring and autumn weeks, most Lukla flights are moved out of Kathmandu to the small airstrip at Manthali in Ramechhap district, to relieve congestion at the capital's single runway. Manthali is four to five hours east of Kathmandu by road.

In practice that gives you two choices for the morning of your flight:

  • Leave Kathmandu around 1 am by shared jeep or tourist bus and go straight to the airstrip.
  • Travel the afternoon before and sleep in a simple hotel in Manthali, which is kinder and means you are at the front of the queue.

The dates of the switch are announced each season and move from year to year, so confirm which airport your ticket is from before you book a hotel for the night before.

Building it into your plan

  • Spare days. Keep one free day at the end of the trek in the main seasons and two or more in the monsoon and its shoulders.
  • International flights. Never book one for the day after you are due out of Lukla.
  • Earliest flight. Ask for the first or second rotation of the day. Later flights are the first to be cancelled.
  • Luggage. Flights have a tight baggage allowance, usually counted as checked plus hand luggage together. Pack your duffel to the limit your airline gives.
  • Cash. If you are stuck you will be paying for extra nights and meals. Carry enough.

When flights are not going

If the delay is a few hours, wait. If flights have been cancelled for the day, your ticket normally rolls to the next available seat, behind passengers already booked for that day. After two or three days of cancellations the backlog can take as long again to clear.

Your options then:

  • Wait in Lukla or Kathmandu. Free, apart from food and a bed.
  • Share a helicopter. Seats are sold per person on five-seat helicopters once fixed-wing flights stop. Helicopters can fly in weather that grounds aeroplanes, but not in all of it, and the price rises with demand. Agree the fare before you get in and pay the operator, not a broker at the fence.
  • Walk out to the road. From Lukla it is two to three days down to the jeep track around Thamdanda or Salleri, then a long day's drive to Kathmandu. Tiring, but certain.
  • Go by road from the start. If you would rather not gamble at all, drive in and add the extra walking days. It is a gentler start for acclimatization too.

Is it safe?

Lukla has a difficult approach and has had accidents, and Nepal's aviation record overall is weaker than that of most countries. The airlines that fly it do so many times a day in season, and the same caution that produces the cancellations is what keeps the operation going. A cancelled flight is the system working. Be patient with it.

The short version

Assume you will lose a day to the Lukla flight. If you do not, you have a free day in Kathmandu at the end, which nobody has ever regretted.
