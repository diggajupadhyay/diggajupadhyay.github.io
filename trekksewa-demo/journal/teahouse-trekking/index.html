Teahouse trekking: what a day and a night are really like

What you sleep in, what you eat, what costs extra and how the day runs on a lodge-to-lodge trek in Nepal.

Most trekking in Nepal is lodge to lodge. You walk from one village to the next and sleep in a family-run guesthouse, known as a teahouse or lodge, so you carry clothes and a sleeping bag rather than a tent and a stove. It is what makes two weeks in the high Himalaya possible for people who have never camped.

The room

A standard room is a small plywood box with two single beds, a foam mattress, a pillow and a blanket. There is usually a hook, a window and a light bulb. There is no heating. Walls are thin enough to hear your neighbour turn over, which is why earplugs appear on every packing list.

Toilets are shared and outside the room on most routes: a squat toilet with a bucket of water lower down, sometimes a seat higher up, nearly always cold. Rooms with an attached bathroom exist on the busiest trails and cost several times more.

Bring a sleeping bag even where blankets are offered. Above 4,000 m the room will be below freezing by morning and one blanket is not enough.

The dining room

Everyone gathers in one room around a stove that is lit in the late afternoon and fed with wood, or dried yak dung above the tree line. This is the only warm place in the building, and it is where you eat, read, charge your phone, meet other trekkers and, if you have a guide, plan the next day.

You are expected to eat dinner and breakfast where you sleep. That is the real business model: rooms are priced low and the kitchen earns the living. Taking a cheap bed and eating elsewhere is bad manners and some lodges will charge you a much higher room rate for it.

The food

Menus are similar everywhere because they were standardised by local lodge committees: dal bhat, fried rice and noodles, soups, momos, potatoes, eggs, pancakes, porridge and chapati.

Dal bhat, rice with lentil soup, vegetable curry and pickle, is the best value and the best fuel. It is cooked fresh in bulk and refills are free. Meat has to be carried up without refrigeration, so most experienced trekkers go vegetarian above the road head.

Prices rise with altitude because every bag of rice arrives on someone's back or on a mule. A meal that costs a few hundred rupees in the valley can cost three times that at the highest lodges. That is not a rip-off. It is freight.

What costs extra

Almost everything beyond the bed and the meal: a hot shower, charging a phone or power bank, Wi-Fi, boiled drinking water and, higher up, sometimes even a second blanket. Charges are per item and climb with height. Carry a power bank, treat your own water and expect to go several days between showers near the top.

Bring enough Nepali rupees in cash for the whole trek. Card machines are rare and cash machines rarer.

The shape of a day

Trekking days start early because mornings are clear and afternoons cloud over. A typical day:

  • 6:00 Tea, then pack.
  • 7:00 Breakfast, ordered the night before.
  • 7:30 Walking, with a tea stop mid-morning.
  • 12:00 Lunch at a lodge on the way, which takes an hour because it is cooked to order.
  • 14:00–15:00 Arrive, choose a room, change into dry clothes.
  • 17:00 The stove is lit. Order dinner.
  • 18:30 Dinner.
  • 20:00 Most people are in their sleeping bags.

Walking days are deliberately short at altitude, often only three to five hours. The afternoon is for resting, short acclimatization walks and drinking tea, not for pushing on to the next village.

A few courtesies

  • Order together and order the same dishes where you can. It saves fuel and gets everyone fed sooner.
  • Do not put rubbish or plastic in the stove.
  • Ask before taking photographs of people or inside kitchens and prayer rooms.
  • Take your shoes off where the family does.
  • In busy seasons, a guide who phones ahead is the difference between a bed and a bench in the dining room.

When there are no lodges

A handful of routes, including the Dhaulagiri Circuit and Upper Dolpo, still need tents, a cook and pack animals. These are organised trips with a registered agency, and they cost and weigh a good deal more. Each trek guide on this site says which kind it is.
