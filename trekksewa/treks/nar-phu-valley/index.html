Nar Phu Valley

A restricted-area loop from Koto up a narrow gorge to the stone villages of Phu and Nar, then over the Kang La at 5,320 m to rejoin the Annapurna Circuit at Ngawal.

Overview

Nar and Phu are two villages in a pair of high valleys north of the Annapurna Circuit, reached through a gorge that leaves the main trail at Koto. The valleys were closed to foreigners until 2002 and still need a restricted-area permit, which keeps numbers low: on most days you meet more yak herders than trekkers. The people are of Tibetan origin, and the villages, Phu on its fortified hill and Nar among barley terraces, have changed far less than Manang on the other side of the ridge.

The usual plan walks in for two long days to Phu, rests there, comes back down to the monastery guesthouse at Nar Phedi, climbs to Nar and then crosses the Kang La to Ngawal in the Manang valley. From there you can drive out, or carry on round the Annapurna Circuit and over the Thorong La, already well acclimatized. Lodges now exist at every stop, so camping is no longer necessary, but they are few and basic and may be shut outside the main seasons.

This is a serious trek. The first walking day climbs almost 1,000 m to sleep at 3,560 m. The pass day takes eight to ten hours, with a 1,200 m climb and a longer, steeper descent. Between Koto and Ngawal there is no road, no clinic and little phone signal. Go with an agency and guide who know the valley, and allow a spare day for weather on the pass.

Highlights

  • Phu, a fortified hill village at the head of its valley
  • Tashi Lhakhang monastery on the hill across from Phu
  • A night in the monastery guesthouse at Nar Phedi
  • Nar village beneath Pisang Peak and Kang Guru
  • The Kang La, with the Annapurna range lined up ahead
  • Days with almost no other trekkers

Strenuous: Long days, basic lodges, no road or rescue post in the valley and a ten-hour crossing of a 5,320 m pass.

Ways to change it

Night at Kang La Phedi

Sleeping at the pastures two to three hours above Nar shortens the pass day. The tea house there is basic and not always open, so check in Nar or carry a tent.

Split the first day at Dharmasala

A simple shelter and tea house in the gorge at about 3,200 m lets you break the climb from Koto to Meta, which is wise if you drove up from Kathmandu the day before.

Continue over the Thorong La

From Ngawal walk to Manang and follow the Annapurna Circuit over the Thorong La to Muktinath and Jomsom. Add five days.

In and out without the pass

When the Kang La is snowbound, return from Nar down the gorge to Koto in two days.

Himlung Base Camp

Add a day in Phu for the long walk up the glacier valley to the base camp of Himlung Himal.

Permits and guide rules

Guide required. Nar Phu is a restricted area. The permit is issued only through a registered trekking agency, and a licensed guide must accompany you throughout. Since March 2026 it can be issued to a single trekker, so you no longer need a second person in order to apply. Permits are checked at the police post in Koto. In practice the guide also arranges the few lodges, which often cannot be reached by phone, and makes the decision on the pass.

Where: Department of Immigration in Kathmandu (the Pokhara immigration office also issues them), through a registered trekking agency only.

Since 22 March 2026 the permit can be issued to a single trekker; before that the minimum was two. You still have to book through a registered agency and walk with a licensed guide, with at most seven trekkers per guide. Checked at Koto. You need an ACAP as well.

ACAP

Conservation area

Annapurna Conservation Area Permit · National Trust for Nature Conservation

Foreign nationalsNPR 3,000person, single entry

Where: Online at NTNC's e-permit site, or in person at the Tourist Service Centre in Bhrikutimandap, Kathmandu, and the Nepal Tourism Board office in Pokhara. A permit issued at a trail checkpost costs double.

The fee includes tax and children under 10 do not need a permit. Paying online by card adds a 2.9% gateway charge. Covers every trek in the Annapurna range and Lower Mustang; Upper Mustang and Nar Phu need a restricted-area permit as well.

Fees and rules change, sometimes mid-season. Treat these as a planning guide and confirm with the Nepal Tourism Board, the Department of Immigration or a registered agency before you travel. Last reviewed 2026-11. All permits and fees

Getting there

Most groups drive from Kathmandu to Besisahar and continue by jeep up the Marsyangdi road to Koto in one long day. The walk ends at Ngawal in the Manang valley, where a jeep can be arranged back down to Besisahar.

  • Bus: Kathmandu to Besisahar · 6–7 hr

    Buses and private vehicles. Agencies usually send a jeep straight through to Koto.

  • Bus: Pokhara to Besisahar · 4–5 hr

    For those starting from Pokhara.

  • Jeep: Besisahar to Koto · 5–6 hr

    A rough road, slow after rain. Koto is the last village before Chame.

  • Jeep: Ngawal to Besisahar · 7–9 hr

    Arranged by your agency. Shared jeeps also leave from Humde and Manang.

Hazards on this route

Altitude far from help

The first walking day gains almost 1,000 m of sleeping height, and the route then stays above 3,400 m for five nights with no road nearby. Take the rest day in Phu, and descend toward Meta or Koto if symptoms get worse. Helicopter evacuation depends on the weather and on getting a message out.

The Kang La

The pass day is eight to ten hours with no shelter above Kang La Phedi. Snow and ice are common on both sides from November to May, and the western side is steep, loose scree. Do not cross in fresh snow or poor visibility; wait a day in Nar.

Gorge trail and rockfall

Between Koto and Meta the path is cut into cliffs above the river, with landslide scars and stones falling after rain. Keep to the inside of the trail and move quickly past the slide areas.

Remoteness

There is no health post, no shop of any size and no reliable phone signal between Koto and Ngawal, and lodges may be closed without notice. Carry a full first-aid kit, water treatment, spare food and enough cash for the whole route.

Practical tips

  • Bring a four-season sleeping bag. Lodge rooms in Phu and Nar are unheated and nights are well below freezing.
  • Carry microspikes and gaiters for the Kang La except in the driest weeks of October.
  • Take all the cash you will need from Kathmandu. There is no bank after Besisahar.
  • Ask before photographing people or the inside of monasteries, and leave a donation at Tashi Lhakhang and Nar Phedi.
  • Your agency needs your passport and visa a working day or two before departure to obtain the permit in Kathmandu.
  • The restricted-area permit is priced by the week, then by the day. Agree the dates with your agency so that a spare weather day is covered.

Quick answers

How long does the Nar Phu Valley trek take?

Most people take 9–14 days. The itinerary on this page is 9 days, including 1 day for rest and acclimatization.

How high does the Nar Phu Valley trek go?

The highest point is Kang La at 5,320 m (17,454 ft). The highest place you sleep is Nar at 4,110 m.

How difficult is it?

We grade it strenuous. Long days, basic lodges, no road or rescue post in the valley and a ten-hour crossing of a 5,320 m pass.

Which permits do I need?

Nar Phu RAP, ACAP. Fees and where to get each one are listed in the permits section above.

Do I need a guide?

Nar Phu is a restricted area. The permit is issued only through a registered trekking agency, and a licensed guide must accompany you throughout. Since March 2026 it can be issued to a single trekker, so you no longer need a second person in order to apply. Permits are checked at the police post in Koto. In practice the guide also arranges the few lodges, which often cannot be reached by phone, and makes the decision on the pass.

When is the best time to go?

May, Oct–Nov are best. Late April to May and late September to mid-November are the reliable windows. The valleys lie in the rain shadow and stay fairly dry in the monsoon, but the road to Koto is often cut by landslides and cloud hides the peaks. From December to March many villagers move down the valley, lodges close and the Kang La is snowbound.
